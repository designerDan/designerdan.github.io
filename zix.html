The business problem

Zix was operating separate softwares, each of them using a different design language, interaction patterns, and information architecture.

Enterprise buyers saw four separate products and had to judge the risk. Would the tools work together? Would staff need separate training? Could Zix support a larger, more complex account? A fragmented experience made the company look less mature at the moment trust mattered most.

What was at stake

Zix was a moving enterprise product organization.

Four products were shipping in parallel, teams had different priorities, and I did not control every roadmap or product decision.

My job was not to redesign everything or approve every screen. It was to create a shared system that helped independent teams make more consistent, customer-centered decisions while the business kept moving.

Decisions that mattered

Build influence into the system

I could not review every screen or control every product decision. Trying to do so would have made design a bottleneck and slowed teams already working against separate priorities. Instead, I created a shared decision framework. The design system included reusable components, documented interaction rules, clear states, and a single source of truth connected to engineering handoffs.

The tradeoff was intentional. I gave up control over some local product choices so teams could move faster with a reliable default for common customer workflows. This was not a visual style guide. It was a way to distribute product judgment across a large organization.

Unify the highest-value workflows first

A complete product redesign was not realistic. The portfolio needed visible progress while each team continued its roadmap work. I used a Top Tasks analysis and a cross-product pattern audit to identify the workflows customers encountered most often. The audit showed that users completed the same core tasks in different ways depending on the product they were using.

We prioritized the patterns with the broadest reach: tables, forms, dashboards, search, filters, bulk actions, error states, and administrative workflows. The tradeoff was that lower-priority inconsistencies remained in place for a time. In return, the program improved the areas customers used most often and gave teams concrete work they could ship in stages.

Improve familiar patterns instead of replacing them

Tables were the dominant interaction pattern across the portfolio. They supported core admin work such as reviewing data, sorting, filtering, managing accounts, and taking action on large sets of records. The problem was not that customers needed a new pattern. The problem was that every team had implemented the same pattern differently.

I chose to standardize and improve tables rather than replace them. The shared pattern defined column behavior, sort and filter rules, bulk actions, readable spacing, accessible target sizes, empty states, success messages, error handling, and undo actions. This reduced unnecessary learning for existing users while creating a more predictable experience across products.

A fragmented product does not get acquired for $860M. A coherent one does.

How I led the work

I began by making the problem visible.

I conducted an audit covering design patterns across 21 applications and showed where similar tasks had drifted into different workflows. I then turned those findings into practical guidance that teams could use. The system was built in Abstract and Sketch, with a single source of truth linked to engineering handoffs. Developers could access current components and interaction rules without relying on stale exports or repeated clarification.

The work also depended on relationships. I worked with engineering teams to understand implementation limits, clarify the intent behind patterns, and help teams apply the system to real product work. My role was to establish shared standards, make the right choice easier, and help teams understand when a workflow required a deliberate exception.

What this demonstrates

This engagement shaped how I lead design in complex organizations.

When product teams are moving in parallel, central control does not scale. The work is to identify the decisions that should be shared, turn them into usable systems, and help teams apply them without waiting for permission. That is how enterprise design becomes durable: not through one designer owning every screen, but through many teams making better decisions with a common foundation.
